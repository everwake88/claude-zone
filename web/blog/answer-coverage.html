What 96% answer coverage actually means

When we deployed a Qatari-Arabic sales agent for IQ Homes — a premium retailer of AI robots, smart home technology and beauty devices — the headline number was 96% answer coverage. Here is what that number means, and why we report it at all.

A demo is not a test

Any agent can be made to look good in a demo. You choose the questions, you know the answers, and the conversation goes where you planned. Real customers do not cooperate. They ask about the product they half-remember from an advert, in dialect, with a spelling nobody planned for, at one in the morning.

Building a real question bank

So we test against real questions. For IQ Homes the test set was 1,180 genuine customer questions, drawn from the conversations the business was already having. Every question was run against the agent and checked: did it answer correctly, from the governed knowledge base, within the rules the brand had set?

96% of them were answered correctly. The remaining 4% are the useful part: each one became either a gap filled in the knowledge base or a rule to hand the question to a person.

Coverage is a property of the knowledge, not the model

The number is high because the knowledge behind the agent is governed. The 23 products in that catalogue each have controlled specifications, policy rules and a never-say list — claims the brand does not permit, blocked at the knowledge layer rather than left to the model's judgement. Detail loads in tiers, so a simple question costs a fraction of a complex one.

A single number, measured against your own customers' questions, beats a good demo every time.

Why we publish it

Because a number can be checked. When a client asks whether the agent is good enough to put in front of their customers, "it performed well in testing" is not an answer. "It answered 96% of 1,180 real questions correctly, and here is what we did about the rest" is.

Every agent we build is tested this way, and the coverage figure is part of the handover.
