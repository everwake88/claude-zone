01 — What it is

Not a keypad menu, and not an FAQ bot

It greets, qualifies, recommends across a price range, walks the customer through whatever choices the product requires, prices the order from your own catalogue, collects and validates the fulfilment details, and produces a confirmed order — in the customer's own dialect, at any hour, across as many conversations at once as arrive.

The distinction that matters is architectural. The agent can describe, compare and recommend, but it cannot set a price, invent a product, skip a required choice or mark an order confirmed. Those are enforced outside the model, which is why the system behaves the same on its ten-thousandth conversation as on its first.

Gold is the selling half of the conversation — reading the buyer, recommending, handling the objection.

Navy is the operational half — the half that ends in a priced, validated order your team can act on without rekeying anything.

02 — How it sells

An explicit sales method, not a script

Answering questions is the easy half. Selling is knowing which question you are actually being asked, and what a good reply to it looks like.

Reads the buyer before it answers

Six recognised buyer states — browsing, decided, undecided, resistant, disengaged, group — each with a different opening move. "I'm hungry" and "how much is the X" get completely different replies.

One qualifying question, then a recommendation

Never two in a row. The second question is the one that loses the sale.

Recommends across a price ladder

Entry, middle and premium in one message, cheapest first, each with a single line on when to pick it — so the buyer places themselves on the range instead of guessing.

Sells combinations, not just items

Two mid-range products often beat one large one on both price and variety — and the agent says the arithmetic out loud, because the arithmetic is the argument.

Respects a stated budget

Anything above it is dropped, not shown "just in case".

Answers price objections without discounting

It reframes the value or moves to a cheaper option in the same family. It never volunteers a discount you did not authorise.

Handles rejection two different ways

If the customer named the problem, it changes category. If they were vague, it offers a better version of the same thing plus two genuine alternatives — and never re-offers what was rejected in another form.

Upsells exactly once

One relevant add-on after each item. Refused once, dropped permanently.

Never pressures

No urgency tactics, no invented scarcity, no repeating a declined offer.

Stays composed under hostility

It does not match tone, over-apologise or defend itself. One short line, back to the task.

03 — Why it holds up

Live conversations are messy. This is engineered for that.

Most ordering bots demo well and fail live, because real people double-send, go quiet, reopen an old chat and change an order after giving their address. These are the mechanisms that make the difference.

Not one message lost

Every inbound message is logged the instant it lands, before any processing. Duplicates are detected and silently ignored.

One conversation, one owner

A locking layer guarantees two messages from the same customer can never be answered twice — the foundation that lets volume scale safely.

Composure under bursts

A short listening window merges rapid-fire messages into a single turn, so the agent answers the whole thought instead of the first fragment.

Dialect-native matching

Arabic spelling variants, diacritics and English product names all resolve to the same item. Three spellings are one thing.

Memory that doesn't scroll

A persistent ledger records what was asked, answered and left half-finished, so long conversations don't lose the thread.

Catalogue as data, not code

Items, prices, options and rules live in one versioned record. A price change goes live in minutes and touches no logic.

Fully auditable

Messages, cart changes, saved details and agent decisions are all stored. Any order can be reconstructed exactly as it happened.

Self-correcting replies

If a reply fails inspection it is rewritten automatically before sending. The customer sees the corrected message and nothing else.

Survives interruptions

A customer who goes quiet mid-order and returns an hour later finds their cart and their details exactly where they left them.

05 — Escalation

Five reasons — and only five — to fetch a human

  • They asked for a person, directly, in either language
  • The question is commercial and beyond the agent's authority
  • The matter is sensitive, or a complaint has escalated
  • The same issue has failed to resolve across turns
  • The request falls outside what the knowledge base governs

When a person is genuinely needed, the conversation moves across with its full history attached. Nothing is retyped and nothing is lost — and the handover is built into the first workflow, not bolted on after launch.
